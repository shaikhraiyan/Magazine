TUMARA TOH SAHI HAI YAAR

“Tumara toh sahi hai yaar… apne hi jeevan ke lage hue hain.” And there it is. That one sentence every medical student has probably heard at least once. From a friend, a relative, a neighbour, or that one uncle who meets you after one year and still asks, “Toh beta, doctor ban gaye?”

“Bas uncle, thoda sa aur.”

This is not going to be another article where a medical student cries about how difficult MBBS is. We all know it is difficult. You know it is difficult. Even the person who made the syllabus probably knew it was difficult. Although I have serious doubts about whether this vast amount of information was ever meant to be retained in these puny brains of humans.

This is about something else.

This is about being a Gen-Z medical student. Because being Gen Z already feels like living life in fast forward.

Everyone is travelling, making reels, going to concerts and finding new cafés. Everyone is dating, gaming, creating content, doing photography, going on random trips and somehow managing to have a life outside their profession.

And then there is us. We open Instagram at 1:17 a.m. and see someone our age saying,

“Guys, thanks to Mahadev, I’m finally an American citizen now!” What? What the fuss?

“American citizenship?”

And here it’s me, jisne abhi tak capital bhi ni dekha, only dreamt of securing a seat at AIIMS Delhi.

One day I’m definitely going to throw away my phone if it keeps on reminding me of my postponed life "coming soon”.

And no, I am not saying medicine is terrible. It isn’t. Most of us genuinely want to become good doctors. We chose this. And honestly, we are proud of it.

There is something special about wearing that white coat. About knowing that one day, someone might come to you at the worst point of their life and trust you to make things better.

That part is worth it.

But sometimes, we do get tired of the price we have to pay for choosing it.

Because while everyone else seems to be entering adulthood, we are still standing at the entrance with a stethoscope in one hand and an admit card in the other.

Take money, for example. That damn friend of 'tumara to Sahi h yaar' had started earning the amount which I might earn after clearing INICET and yes, Pranam to Modi Ji for 8th pay commission, if it comes into effect.

For now the greatest investment made by me is the Marrow subscription that I bought last year.

“Papa: UPI kar du?”

Many of us are still being financially supported by the same people who have been paying for our lives since childhood. And yes, we know how lucky we are.

But there is still a small dream that a lot of young men have, i.e. buying a bike with his own money.

Not because we desperately need another bike. But because that bike would quietly say,

“I bought this.”

No father’s money. No mother’s savings. No “I’ll pay you back.”

Just you, your salary and probably an EMI that will follow you for the next three years.

For a medical student, that moment feels very far away.

At this point, the bike is no longer a vehicle; it is a long-term academic project.

And then there are our parents. They have dreams for us that we didn’t exactly ask for.

They want us to become successful. We want that too. They want us to have a good marriage, a nice house, financial security and a respectable life.

Honestly, we want most of that too. But who knows what lies in the future, a good/stable marriage or a 'BLUE DRUM'?

And then there’s the beautiful little monster called the competitive exam.

We don’t just study medicine anymore. We study medicine while thinking about the next exam. Every good day has an asterisk.

“I studied really well today.” Nice. “But how much of this will actually help in INI-CET?”

Every bad day has an even bigger asterisk.

“I wasted the whole day." Congratulations. “Now what about INI-CET?”

The exam hasn’t even happened yet, and somehow it has already ruined several evenings.

And the funniest part? We are preparing for an exam whose reward is…

“MORE YEARS OF STUDYING”

It’s almost like the game designers forgot to add the ending.

MBBS → NEET-PG/INI-CET → Residency → Fellowship → Superspeciality → Consultant.

At some point, someone should probably ask.

“When exactly do we unlock the life expansion pack?”

Dating deserves its own paragraph.

Because apparently being a medical student is not enough of a full-time commitment. You are also expected to maintain a relationship.

Now imagine this.

“Hey, want to go out tonight?” “Sure.”

“When?”

“After posting.” “Okay, tomorrow?” “Unit test.” “Weekend?” “Family function.” “Next week?” “Pre-PG preparation.”

“Okay. When are you actually free?”

And then you realise you have been in a relationship with your timetable for six years.

At least the timetable is toxic but consistent.

And perhaps the funniest part is that none of this is visible from the outside.

To everyone else, you’re doing great. You’re a medical student. You’re going to become a doctor. You’ll earn well. You’ll be respected. You’ll have a stable career.

Basically, you’ve “made it". Hushhhh

What I think maybe the real problem is not that we are missing out. Maybe it is that we are constantly aware that we are missing out.

Gen millennials didn’t have Instagram showing them what everyone else was doing at two in the morning. We do. We compare them.

Someone is somehow 23, financially independent, travelling the world and maintaining visible abs.

And we are sitting in the library at 11:45 p.m. trying to remember whether the answer was hydrocele or hernia.

social media has given our generation the unique privilege of feeling behind while we are still running.

But maybe this is the part we don’t talk about enough. Maybe we are not actually behind. Maybe our timelines are just different.

The friend who started earning at 21 may have reached financial independence earlier; we may reach it later.

The person who travelled at 20 may have memories we don’t have, but we may have experiences they don’t.

The person who bought a bike at 22 may have bought it with his first salary.

Maybe we will buy ours at 28, with our own name on the payslip. And maybe that will make it even more special.

Because it was never about the bike.

Maybe one day, we might actually become everything we spent our youth preparing to become.

And when that day comes, I just hope we don’t look back and realise that we were so busy preparing for life that we forgot to have one.

Even though I hope if you ask the person standing outside all of this, he will probably still look at me, smile and say,

“Tumara toh sahi hai yaar.”
